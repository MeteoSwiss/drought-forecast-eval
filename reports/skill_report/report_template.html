How skilful are the weekly drought forecasts?

This report verifies the 0–3 week forecasts of the drought indicators against observations. The first part scores the CDI and its sub-indices as drought levels 1–5. The second scores the precipitation sums, the SPI and soil moisture as numbers, using the full p10/p50/p90 forecast. Meteograms show the forecasts behind the scores, region by region. The benchmarks are persistence (last week's observation carried forward) and the 1991–2020 climatology.

Part 1 · CDI and sub-indices, p50 as drought level

Drought levels

Key findings

  1. The CDI forecast beats persistence for about one week. At lead week 0, the Gerrity skill score is 0.79 against 0.64 for persistence. At lead week 1 it is 0.60 against 0.54, which is not significant over the full record but is significant within the 2026 drought season (0.45 against 0.26). At lead week 2 the two are level. At lead week 3 the forecast is significantly worse (0.34 against 0.48).
  2. The forecast median becomes too mild as lead time grows. For the CDI, the share of forecasts at least one level too mild rises from 16% to 38% between lead weeks 0 and 3. The share that is too severe stays near 10%. The frequency bias for CDI ≥ 3 falls from 0.97 to 0.62. Warnings are rarely false alarms (FAR 0.11–0.19), but by lead week 3 about half of the level-3+ weeks are missed (POD 0.51 against 0.65 for persistence). Soil moisture shows the same pattern more strongly (bias 0.28 at lead week 3).
  3. The precipitation indices carry the added value. For the 1-month index, which persistence handles badly, the forecast is better through lead week 2 (Gerrity 0.47 against 0.23 at lead week 1). The 24-month index is ahead of persistence at every lead (0.81 against 0.65 at lead week 3). Over the full record that gain is significant only at lead week 3, but within the 2026 season it is significant at every lead. The 3-month index is significantly better through lead week 1.
  4. The hydrological index never beats persistence, and it over-warns. It is level with persistence at lead weeks 0–1 and significantly worse from lead week 2 (0.31 against 0.50). It is the only index with a frequency bias above 1 (1.17–1.23 for level ≥ 3), and 13–18% of non-drought weeks get a false onset warning, against 0–6% for the other indices.
  5. Forecasts do anticipate drought onsets, which persistence cannot. Of the region-weeks where the CDI moved from below level 3 to level 3 or above, the forecast caught 68% at lead week 0, 52% at lead week 1, 35% at lead week 2 and 26% at lead week 3, with false onsets in 3–5% of cases.

The prose quotes the full record. The charts follow the period switch at the top. Almost all level-3+ drought in the record falls in April–September 2026, so in effect this verifies one drought season. Weeks and regions are strongly correlated, so treat the intervals as the honest measure of certainty, not the sample size.

At a glance

Where the forecast beats persistence

Verdicts use the Gerrity skill score difference (forecast minus persistence) and its 90% block-bootstrap interval. “Better” and “worse” mean the interval excludes zero.

Multi-category skill

Skill against lead time

The Gerrity skill score (GSS) is the standard equitable score for ordered categories. A constant or random forecast scores 0 and a perfect one scores 1. A miss by one level costs less than a miss by three, and it pays more for getting a rare level right than a common one. The Heidke score (HSS) gives credit only for an exact match. PC is the proportion correct, and PC±1 counts forecasts within one level.

This is the Gerrity skill score of the forecast minus that of persistence, scored on the same cases. Bars show the 90% interval from a moving-block bootstrap over issue weeks, with all 38 regions resampled together. Filled dots mark intervals that exclude zero.

Warning thresholds

Yes/no scores for “level ≥ k”

Each threshold turns the forecast into a yes/no warning. The table pairs forecast with persistence, and the better value of each pair is set in bold. ETS is the equitable threat score. SEDI (symmetric extremal dependence index) does not shrink to zero when an event is rare, so it compares fairly between thresholds and periods. The last column is the 90% interval of the ETS gain over persistence.

Error structure

Too mild or too severe?

This is the share of all forecasts that put the region at a lower level than observed (too mild) or a higher level (too severe). The gap between the two sides is the systematic part of the error. For most indices it grows on the “too mild” side with lead time.

Contingency tables

These are the full 5 × 5 tables behind the scores. Rows are the forecast level and columns the observed level. Cells on the diagonal are exact hits, and the shading shows each cell’s share of all cases.

Changes of level

Onsets and transitions

Persistence is right whenever nothing changes, so a forecast only adds value when the level moves. These numbers count only the region-weeks where the observed level differs from the last observation available at issue time. An onset is a move from below level 3 to level 3 or above.

Part 2 · precipitation sums, SPI and soil moisture, p10/p50/p90

Numerical variables

Key findings

  1. All precipitation sums have the same absolute error, and the 1-month sum is the honest measure. At each lead week the p50 error is identical for every window from 1 to 24 months: a mean absolute error of 11, 22, 30 and 37 mm at lead weeks 0–3. It is the error of the same one to four forecast weeks. The long windows score higher skill only because most of each window is already observed. For the 1-month sum, the MAE skill against climatology falls from 0.74 to 0.15 between lead weeks 0 and 3, and against persistence from 0.51 to 0.27. Both are significant at every lead.
  2. The forecast was too wet during the 2026 drought. At lead week 3 in the 2026 season, 83% of observed 1-month sums fell below the forecast median (50% expected), and the p50 was 31 mm (38%) too high. Over the full record the wet bias at lead week 3 is +11 mm (12%). The SPI shows the same thing (+0.65 for SPI-1 at lead week 3 in the season).
  3. The p10–p90 range is a little too narrow on the dry side. It holds 66–76% of observations instead of 80%. The misses fall mostly below p10 (17–20% of cases instead of 10%) and much less often above p90 (7–14%). At lead week 0 the range is very narrow, a fifth of the climatological range for the 1-month sum, and it covers only 66%.
  4. Soil moisture is badly calibrated. Only 31–51% of observations fall inside the p10–p90 range, 39–51% fall below p10, and the p50 is 1.5–9 percentage points too wet. It beats persistence at lead weeks 0–1 (MAE skill 0.53 and 0.27), but not at lead weeks 2–3 (0.06 and −0.05, both not significant).
  5. Dry-decile weeks are well discriminated. For the event “below the climatological 10th percentile”, the forecast probability for the 1-month sum has a Brier skill score against climatology of 0.77, 0.51, 0.39 and 0.27, and a ROC area of 0.98, 0.91, 0.87 and 0.81 at lead weeks 0–3. All four Brier skill scores are significant.

Method

Scores and set-up

Sample
Monday issues only, as in verification_summer2026.ipynb. Observations are weekly, also on Mondays. Lead week 0 is valid on the issue Monday itself. Scores pool all regions and issue dates for each lead week.
Persistence
The last observation strictly before the issue date, held flat over all leads (persistence_reference). At lead week k it is therefore k + 1 weeks old.
Climatology
The 1991–2020 p10/p50/p90 for the same region and week of the year, ±1 week (climatology_reference). It is used for the numerical variables only. For the drought levels its median is level 1 almost everywhere, so a climatology forecast would score exactly 0 on every equitable score.
GSS
Gerrity (1992) skill score. Scoring weights come from the observed level frequencies in the sample being scored, and they are recomputed for every bootstrap resample. Levels never observed (level 5 of the 24-month index) are merged into the nearest observed level.
HSS · PSS
Multi-category Heidke and Peirce skill scores. They are equitable but give credit only for an exact level match.
POD · FAR · FBI
Hit rate, false alarm ratio and frequency bias (predicted ÷ observed events) for the yes/no event “level ≥ k”.
ETS · SEDI
Equitable threat score, and the symmetric extremal dependence index of Ferro & Stephenson (2011). SEDI does not depend on how often the event happens.
MAE skill
1 − MAE(p50) ÷ MAE(reference), where the reference is persistence or the climatological median.
Quantile skill
1 − QS(forecast) ÷ QS(climatology), where QS is the pinball loss averaged over p10, p50 and p90. It scores the whole three-quantile forecast, spread included.
Dry decile
The event “observation below the climatological p10 for that region and week”. The forecast probability is read from a piecewise-linear CDF through (p10, 0.1), (p50, 0.5) and (p90, 0.9), extended linearly beyond. The Brier skill score is relative to climatology, which always gives 0.1.
Intervals
A moving-block bootstrap over issue weeks (blocks of 4 weeks, 2 000 resamples), resampling all regions together. Forecast and reference are resampled as pairs.

Caveats

  • There is one drought season in the record. Spring and summer 2026 dominate every level-3+ statistic, and the July 2025 – April 2026 period has very few such events (base rate under 10%). Its scores are correspondingly unstable.
  • Some of the lead-week-0 gain is nowcasting. The forecast is valid on the issue date, while persistence is a week old.
  • The drought levels have only a p50 in the archive, so they are judged on the median alone. A median drifting toward “no drought” at longer leads is expected behaviour, and not necessarily a flaw of the ensemble.
  • Skill against climatology rises with the accumulation window mainly because more of the window is observed. Compare windows on absolute error, or against persistence.
  • The issue date 2026-07-20 is missing from the archive.
